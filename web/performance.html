Performance

How SQPOPT does on test problems: first one small enough to draw, then the 305 small problems of the Hock–Schittkowski collection, 664 small problems of the CUTEst collection next to SciPy's solvers, large sparse problems with up to a million variables, and 17 bound-constrained test functions of any size.

The options mentioned here are described in the user guide, and Choosing settings measures the effect of the QP solver and Hessian settings on problems of different sizes, sparsity, and degrees of freedom. All times are for a release build (--profile release) on one core of an Apple M-series laptop.

A small example

The Rosenbrock function on the unit disk, a problem with two variables and one constraint (test/test_rosenbrock_disk.f90):

\[ \min_{x,\,y} \; 100\,(y - x^2)^2 + (1 - x)^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad x^2 + y^2 \le 1 \]

The function's curved valley leads to its unconstrained minimizer, \( (1, 1) \), which is outside the disk, so the constraint is active at the solution, \( (0.7864, 0.6177) \), where the objective is 0.0457. The solve starts at \( (-1.2, 1) \), the usual starting point of the Rosenbrock function, which is outside the disk too. With the default options it converges in 12 steps, with 15 evaluations of the functions:

  • The first step crosses the plane. The first QP has only the gradient to go on (the L-BFGS matrix starts as the identity), so its step is long. The point it reaches has a lower objective, and that is enough for the filter to accept it, although it is further outside the disk.
  • Iterations 2 to 5 come back to the valley and into the disk, as the quasi-Newton matrix learns the curvature. Iteration 4 is still just outside the circle, and iteration 5 is well inside.
  • Iterations 6 to 9 follow the valley's floor toward \( (1, 1) \), with the constraint inactive, until iteration 9 lands slightly outside the circle.
  • The last three iterations converge on the circle, with the constraint active: the violation falls from \( 3 \times 10^{-3} \) at iteration 9 to \( 2 \times 10^{-6} \) and then \( 5 \times 10^{-12} \).

Hock–Schittkowski problems

The collection of Hock and Schittkowski has 305 small problems (2 to 100 variables) with documented solutions. It is the solver's main regression test (test/test_hs_suite.f90): each problem is solved from its standard starting point, and counted as solved if the final point is feasible and its objective is within \( 10^{-4} \) (relative) of the documented optimum, as local if the solver converged to a different local solution, and as failed otherwise.

The merit-function rows are without interpolation, except where noted. With interpolation, Armijo / \( \ell_1 \) / multipliers solves two more problems (275), with 22,322 fc calls instead of 18,627. Analytic derivatives are used throughout, except for the 16 problems for which the collection provides none (they are solved with finite-difference derivatives, as the collection intends); the analytic derivatives that were wrong in the original collection are fixed in test/schittkowski_problems.f90.

The collection has no second derivatives, so the exact-Hessian rows use Hessians of the Lagrangian computed by the test harness by central differences of the analytic gradients (the 16 problems without analytic derivatives keep BFGS). On the problems that both it and the default solve, the exact Hessian needs 30% fewer iterations (median), and far fewer on some (TP116: 25 instead of 127); its larger fc total comes from a few problems where Newton steps crawl along a curved valley. Inertia control with MUMPS removes most of that difference; with QDLDL, the default sparse solver, it solves 3 more problems too, but with more evaluations, since without pivoting QDLDL can't tell the inertia of a Hessian with zeros on its diagonal, and shifts it more than needed (see Sparse solver). Every problem

Every one of the 305 problems in Schittkowski's collection, solved by SQPOPT from its standard starting point, next to the published results of Schittkowski's NLPQLP, and to SLSQP run on the same problems. A problem is solved if the final point is feasible and its objective is within the tolerance of the validated optimum (or better), local if the solver converged to a feasible point with a worse objective (a different local solution), and failed otherwise. CUTEst problems

CUTEst is the standard collection of test problems for nonlinear optimization. This is a first run of SQPOPT on its small problems, next to the two general solvers of SciPy: SLSQP (an SQP method, in Fortran) and trust-constr (a trust-region interior-point method, in Python). All three are called from Python, SQPOPT through its Python interface, and the problems come from PyCUTEst.

The set is the 664 problems with at most 100 variables and 100 constraints whose sizes are fixed in CUTEst's classification. 234 of them have no constraints other than bounds, 286 have constraints but no more than variables, and 144 have more constraints than variables (most of these are data-fitting problems written as one equation per data point, which can't all hold). Each solver gets the problem's starting point, analytic first derivatives, at most 250 iterations, and no second derivatives.

  • SQPOPT solves the most problems: 526 of 664 (79%), against 493 for SLSQP and 396 for trust-constr. 32 problems are solved by SQPOPT alone, 16 by SLSQP alone, and 3 by trust-constr alone. No solver solves 115 of them.
  • The difference is on the constrained problems. Without constraints SLSQP solves four more than SQPOPT. With them, SQPOPT solves 323 and SLSQP 286.
  • SLSQP needs slightly fewer evaluations. On the 368 problems that all three solve, SLSQP makes 22,409 calls of the objective, gradient, constraint, and Jacobian functions, SQPOPT 24,273, and trust-constr 49,696. The time for those problems, with the functions called from Python, is 0.6 s, 1.2 s, and 8.0 s.
  • SQPOPT always returned. Each problem runs in a process of its own with a 60-second limit. That process crashed or reached the limit on 18 problems, in one of the SciPy solvers each time, after SQPOPT had returned its result. trust-constr also raised an error on 92 problems, most of them with more equality constraints than variables, which it doesn't accept.

  • The tolerances are not the reason. SQPOPT's defaults (ktol = 1e-6, ctol = 1e-8) are tighter than the other solvers' settings here. With the tolerances of a published benchmark (ktol = 1.22e-4, ctol = 2e-6), only 3 of the 138 are solved.
  • More constraints than variables. Every QP subproblem of such a problem is inconsistent, so SQPOPT solves it by Gauss-Newton steps on the constraint violation alone (see Feasibility restoration). 58 of the 144 have a point that satisfies all the constraints, which it finds; for most of the others the status sqpopt_infeasible is the answer. The 22 that reach the iteration limit and the 12 that end with no acceptable step are badly conditioned fits, on which those steps converge slowly or find no decrease. Problems of this kind are better given to the least-squares interface, which is not what was measured here.
  • The 23 that another solver solves are the ones to study: 20 that reach the iteration limit (among them 3PK, ALLINITC, GAUSS2LS, HIMMELBJ, and MAXLIKA), and ALLINITA, HS13, and HS99EXP.

How these were measured

With tools/cutest_benchmark.py, which needs the CUTEst library, the SIF decoder, and the SIF problem files (its setup command downloads what is missing, about 4 GB, into a directory that is not in the repository):

pixi run build-python     # the Python interface's extension
pixi run cutest setup     # once
pixi run cutest run       # solve the problems (an interrupted run is continued)
pixi run cutest report --failures=sqpopt
  • Versions: CUTEst 2.7.1, SIFDecode 3.1.1, PyCUTEst 1.8.2, SciPy 1.18.1, and the SIF collection of October 2026.
  • Settings: SQPOPT with its defaults and max_iter = 250; SLSQP with maxiter = 250 and ftol = 1e-6; trust-constr with maxiter = 250, gtol = 2e-5, and xtol = 2e-100 (its quasi-Newton Hessian). The SciPy settings are those of Joshy and Hwang's comparison of SQP solvers on CUTEst (OpenSQP, 2025).
  • What this isn't: a comparison with SNOPT or IPOPT, which were not run; and it isn't the set of that paper, which took the 575 problems of an older collection whose default sizes are within the limits. The problems whose size is a parameter are left out here.

Scalable test functions

How SQPOPT does on 17 standard test functions that are defined for any number of variables, from 20 to 100,000. They have bounds on the variables and no other constraints, so they test the bound-constrained part of the solver at scale.

The functions come from the Julia package NonlinearOptimizationTestFunctions.jl (MIT license), which collects them from the literature. They were converted to Fortran in test/scalable_functions.f90, and test/test_scalable.f90 solves them. All times are for a release build (--profile release), one run each, on one core of an Apple M-series laptop; the factorizations use QDLDL, the default sparse solver.

Summary

  • With second derivatives, the solver handles these functions at every size tried. With the exact Hessian and the direct QP method, every function with 100,000 variables except rosenbrock is solved in under half a second.
  • Without them, the default L-BFGS Hessian needs many more iterations on ill-conditioned functions: thousands with 1,000 variables, in up to 8 seconds.
  • The runs led to three changes in the solver: a bug fix (the direct QP method rejected very long steps), a much cheaper iteration for problems without active constraints, and two safeguards for badly scaled starting points. Two weaknesses remain (the dense QP at its largest size, and the accuracy reached from a starting point with a large gradient). They are described under What the runs show.

Each solve starts from the function's standard starting point, and its result is one of:

resultmeaning
(no label)converged, with an objective within \( 10^{-5} \max(1, |f^*|) \) of the known global minimum \( f^* \)
looseconverged, and the distance to the minimum was reduced by a factor of \( 10^{8} \) or more, but the objective is not that close to it
localconverged to another stationary point, on a function that has other local minimizers
failedthe solver did not report convergence

"Converged" means a status of sqpopt_success, sqpopt_acceptable, or sqpopt_stalled, at a point within the bounds with a smaller objective than the start's. SQPOPT is a local method: on the functions with many local minimizers it is only expected to find one of them.

What the runs show

  • L-BFGS needs many iterations on ill-conditioned functions, but they are cheap. With 1,000 variables, trid (whose Hessian has a condition number of \( 4 \times 10^{5} \)) takes 1,715 iterations and rosenbrock 4,946, in 2.6 and 7.8 seconds. With the exact Hessian they take 23 and 1,704 iterations, and under half a second. If you have second derivatives of a large problem, use them.
  • These runs led to the unconstrained step. At first the L-BFGS solves were 10 to 16 times slower (42 and 78 seconds for the two functions above), with nearly all of the time in the QP solver, which handled a QP with no active constraint like any other. Now the quasi-Newton step is tried first, and it is the QP's solution whenever it satisfies the bounds. The iteration counts did not change, and they are those of L-BFGS itself: scipy's L-BFGS-B takes 1,502 iterations on rosenbrock with 300 variables, and SQPOPT 1,524 (python/tests/test_scipy_compare.py).
  • Roundoff in the objective can stop the line search. trid was first evaluated as its definition reads, the difference of two sums. Near the minimizer with 1,000 variables each sum is about \( 3 \times 10^{13} \) and their difference \( -1.7 \times 10^{8} \), so the value had an error of about 0.1. That is more than a quasi-Newton step gains near the solution, so no step length was acceptable, and the solve ended with "line search failed" at a relative distance of \( 4 \times 10^{-8} \) from the minimum. Evaluated term by term, without the cancellation, the function is solved. If a solve of yours ends that way close to a solution, look at how accurately the objective is computed.
  • A bug in the direct QP method was found and fixed. trid with 100,000 variables did not finish: its first QP was handed to the active-set solver, which can't cope with 100,000 free variables. The direct method had computed the right step, but rejected it as inaccurate. The first Newton step of this function is about \( 10^{9} \) times its gradient, so the stationarity residual \( g + Hp \) is a difference of very large terms, and its roundoff was compared with the size of the gradient instead of the size of those terms. With the test corrected, the solve takes 0.4 seconds.
  • The dense QP solver is slow at the largest size it is given. The automatic choice uses the dense QP for up to 200 variables. With 200 variables, brown takes 0.8 seconds for 9 iterations, nearly all of it in the few QPs whose unconstrained step leaves the bounds, against 0.001 seconds with 1,000 variables and the sparse QP. For problems of 100 to 200 variables with few active constraints, set options%qp_solver_mode = sqpopt_qp_reduced_hessian (see Which QP solver).
  • A starting point with a very large gradient can give a loose solution. The objective is scaled by its gradient at the starting point (see Scaling), and convergence is tested on the scaled problem. Two functions stopped far from their minimum of 0, with the status sqpopt_acceptable, while their objectives were still falling fast: zakharov (starting gradient \( 3 \times 10^{19} \)) at \( f = 5 \times 10^{6} \), and schwefel12 (\( 5 \times 10^{7} \)) at 593. That led to two safeguards: a limit on the scale factor (options%scaling_min_value, as in IPOPT), with which zakharov reaches \( 8 \times 10^{-7} \), and a test on the objective's change in the acceptable-level stop (options%acceptable_obj_change_tol), with which schwefel12 reaches 0.56. Those are still marked loose where they miss the test's tolerance; with options%scaling = .false. both functions are solved to roundoff, schwefel12 in 7 iterations.
  • The chained Rosenbrock function needs a number of iterations in proportion to its size from this starting point: about 1.7 per variable with the exact Hessian (98 with 20 variables, 1,704 with 1,000, 16,887 with 10,000) and about 5 per variable with L-BFGS (491 with 100, 1,973 with 400, 4,946 with 1,000). That is a property of the function, whose variables have to follow a curved valley one after the other, and not of this solver: scipy's L-BFGS-B needs about 5 per variable too. So raise options%max_iter for a problem like this (the default is 100).

How these were measured

test_scalable first checks every gradient and Hessian by central differences. Run without options, it solves every function with 20 and 1,000 variables, and fails if a solve doesn't converge. With options it runs other sizes and reports the results:

# the default run (a regression test)
pixi run fpm test test_scalable

# the tables above
pixi run fpm test test_scalable --profile release -- --n=1000
pixi run fpm test test_scalable --profile release -- --n=10000 --hessian=exact
pixi run fpm test test_scalable --profile release -- --n=10000 --hessian=direct --max-iter=40000
pixi run fpm test test_scalable --profile release -- --n=100000 --hessian=direct --function=sphere

The options are --n=N1,N2,... (multiples of 4), --function=NAME, --hessian=bfgs|exact|direct, --linear-solver=qdldl|mumps (mumps with pixi run test-mumps), --max-iter=K, and --print=L. These functions have no constraints other than bounds. For constrained problems, see the large problems and the Hock–Schittkowski problems above.
