Scalable test functions

How SQPOPT does on 17 standard test functions that are defined for any number of variables, from 20 to 100,000. They have bounds on the variables and no other constraints, so they test the bound-constrained part of the solver at scale.

The functions come from the Julia package NonlinearOptimizationTestFunctions.jl (MIT license), which collects them from the literature. They were converted to Fortran in test/scalable_functions.f90, and test/test_scalable.f90 solves them. All times are for a release build (--profile release) with MUMPS, one run each, on one core of an Apple M-series laptop.

Summary

  • With second derivatives, the solver handles these functions at every size tried. With the exact Hessian and the direct QP method, most functions with 100,000 variables are solved in about 2 seconds or less.
  • Without them, the default L-BFGS Hessian needs many more iterations on ill-conditioned functions: thousands with 1,000 variables, in up to 8 seconds.
  • The runs led to three changes in the solver: a bug fix (the direct QP method rejected very long steps), a much cheaper iteration for problems without active constraints, and two safeguards for badly scaled starting points. Two weaknesses remain (the dense QP at its largest size, and the accuracy reached from a starting point with a large gradient). They are described under What the runs show.

Each solve starts from the function's standard starting point, and its result is one of:

resultmeaning
(no label)converged, with an objective within \( 10^{-5} \max(1, |f^*|) \) of the known global minimum \( f^* \)
looseconverged, and the distance to the minimum was reduced by a factor of \( 10^{8} \) or more, but the objective is not that close to it
localconverged to another stationary point, on a function that has other local minimizers
failedthe solver did not report convergence

"Converged" means a status of sqpopt_success, sqpopt_acceptable, or sqpopt_stalled, at a point within the bounds with a smaller objective than the start's. SQPOPT is a local method: on the functions with many local minimizers it is only expected to find one of them.

The bounds are those of the package (for example \( \pm 5.12 \) for sphere, and \( \pm n^2 \) for trid). The gradients were converted from the package and the Hessians derived by hand; the test checks both by central differences. Two starting points differ from the package's: qing (the package starts at a stationary point) and schumer_steiglitz (the package's start is random). The package's exponential function was left out: at large \( n \) its gradient underflows to zero at the starting point.

What the runs show

  • L-BFGS needs many iterations on ill-conditioned functions, but they are cheap. With 1,000 variables, trid (whose Hessian has a condition number of \( 4 \times 10^{5} \)) takes 1,715 iterations and rosenbrock 4,946, in 2.6 and 7.8 seconds. With the exact Hessian they take 23 and 1,704 iterations, and under half a second. If you have second derivatives of a large problem, use them.
  • These runs led to the unconstrained step. At first the L-BFGS solves were 10 to 16 times slower (42 and 78 seconds for the two functions above), with nearly all of the time in the QP solver, which handled a QP with no active constraint like any other. Now the quasi-Newton step is tried first, and it is the QP's solution whenever it satisfies the bounds. The iteration counts did not change, and they are those of L-BFGS itself: scipy's L-BFGS-B takes 1,502 iterations on rosenbrock with 300 variables, and SQPOPT 1,524 (python/tests/test_scipy_compare.py).
  • Roundoff in the objective can stop the line search. trid was first evaluated as its definition reads, the difference of two sums. Near the minimizer with 1,000 variables each sum is about \( 3 \times 10^{13} \) and their difference \( -1.7 \times 10^{8} \), so the value had an error of about 0.1. That is more than a quasi-Newton step gains near the solution, so no step length was acceptable, and the solve ended with "line search failed" at a relative distance of \( 4 \times 10^{-8} \) from the minimum. Evaluated term by term, without the cancellation, the function is solved. If a solve of yours ends that way close to a solution, look at how accurately the objective is computed.
  • A bug in the direct QP method was found and fixed. trid with 100,000 variables did not finish: its first QP was handed to the active-set solver, which can't cope with 100,000 free variables. The direct method had computed the right step, but rejected it as inaccurate. The first Newton step of this function is about \( 10^{9} \) times its gradient, so the stationarity residual \( g + Hp \) is a difference of very large terms, and its roundoff was compared with the size of the gradient instead of the size of those terms. With the test corrected, the solve takes 2.2 seconds.
  • The dense QP solver is slow at the largest size it is given. The automatic choice uses the dense QP for up to 200 variables. With 200 variables, brown takes 0.8 seconds for 9 iterations, nearly all of it in the few QPs whose unconstrained step leaves the bounds, against 0.001 seconds with 1,000 variables and the sparse QP. For problems of 100 to 200 variables with few active constraints, set options%qp_solver_mode = sqpopt_qp_reduced_hessian (see Which QP solver).
  • A starting point with a very large gradient can give a loose solution. The objective is scaled by its gradient at the starting point (see Scaling), and convergence is tested on the scaled problem. Two functions stopped far from their minimum of 0, with the status sqpopt_acceptable, while their objectives were still falling fast: zakharov (starting gradient \( 3 \times 10^{19} \)) at \( f = 5 \times 10^{6} \), and schwefel12 (\( 5 \times 10^{7} \)) at 593. That led to two safeguards: a limit on the scale factor (options%scaling_min_value, as in IPOPT), with which zakharov reaches \( 8 \times 10^{-7} \), and a test on the objective's change in the acceptable-level stop (options%acceptable_obj_change_tol), with which schwefel12 reaches 0.56. Those are still marked loose where they miss the test's tolerance; with options%scaling = .false. both functions are solved to roundoff, schwefel12 in 7 iterations.
  • The chained Rosenbrock function needs a number of iterations in proportion to its size from this starting point: about 1.7 per variable with the exact Hessian (98 with 20 variables, 1,704 with 1,000, 16,887 with 10,000) and about 5 per variable with L-BFGS (491 with 100, 1,973 with 400, 4,946 with 1,000). That is a property of the function, whose variables have to follow a curved valley one after the other, and not of this solver: scipy's L-BFGS-B needs about 5 per variable too. So raise options%max_iter for a problem like this (the default is 100).

How these were measured

test_scalable first checks every gradient and Hessian by central differences. Run without options, it solves every function with 20 and 1,000 variables, and fails if a solve doesn't converge. With options it runs other sizes and reports the results:

# the default run (a regression test)
pixi run fpm test test_scalable

# the tables above, in a build with MUMPS
pixi run test-mumps test_scalable --profile release -- --n=1000
pixi run test-mumps test_scalable --profile release -- --n=10000 --hessian=exact
pixi run test-mumps test_scalable --profile release -- --n=10000 --hessian=direct
pixi run test-mumps test_scalable --profile release -- --n=100000 --hessian=direct --function=sphere

The options are --n=N1,N2,... (multiples of 4), --function=NAME, --hessian=bfgs|exact|direct, --max-iter=K, and --print=L. These functions have no constraints other than bounds. For constrained problems, see the large problems of the guide's Performance section and the Hock–Schittkowski results.
