Choosing settings

The defaults work for most problems. This page explains when to change them, based on measurements: the 305 Hock–Schittkowski problems (small, dense) and families of large sparse problems with known solutions, solved with every relevant setting.

The settings mentioned here are described in full in the guide's configuration reference. All times are for a release build (--profile release) on one core of an Apple M-series laptop. Only the ratios between them matter.

Two settings are almost never worth changing: keep the sparse QP's null_space = sqpopt_null_space_lu (the LSQR alternative was slower on all but two of the tests here, by up to 200× on the larger problems) and warm_start = .true. (a cold start took up to 5× longer).

First, find where the time goes

An SQP solve spends its time in two places: your functions (one call of fc and usually one of gjac per iteration, more when steps are rejected), and the QP subproblems (one or more per iteration). Which one dominates decides what to tune:

  • If your functions are expensive (a simulation, say), only the number of iterations matters: reduce it with a better Hessian (exact), better scaling, or a better starting point. The QP settings hardly matter.
  • If your functions are cheap, the QP usually dominates, and the choice of QP solver can change the run time by a factor of 100 (below).

The results report the split: results%time is the total, and results%time_functions and results%time_qp are the parts spent in your functions and in the QP. With options%print_level = 1 the final summary prints them too.

Which QP solver

options%qp_solver_mode = sqpopt_qp_auto (the default) uses the dense QP for problems with at most qp_solver%auto_dense_max_n = 200 variables, and the sparse QP above. The dense QP refactorizes dense matrices whenever its working set changes, so its cost grows roughly as \( n^3 \) per QP iteration; the sparse QP's grows with the number of nonzeros and the fill-in of the LU factors.

  • Up to about 50 variables, both QPs take milliseconds, and the dense one needs slightly fewer function evaluations (on the HS problems and the dense family). The default does the right thing.
  • From about 50 to 200 variables, the default still picks the dense QP, but the sparse QP is faster: about 2–6× on the fully dense problem, and up to 100× on the sparse ones. If your functions are cheap, choose the sparse QP explicitly, or lower the switch-over point: 
    options%qp_solver_mode = sqpopt_qp_reduced_hessian    ! always the sparse QP
! or:
qp_solver%auto_dense_max_n = 50                       ! the sparse QP above 50 variables
call solver%initialize(problem=problem, options=options, qp_solver=qp_solver)
    If your functions are expensive, the dense QP's slightly better steps may still be worth its cost at this size; compare results%n_eval_fc both ways.
  • Above 200 variables, use the sparse QP (the default). The dense QP stores n×n and m×n arrays and quickly becomes impractical.
  • Keep the LU null space. LSQR was 2–200× slower than LU on the sparse problems here, and the gap grows with the size. (It was faster only on the small, dense HS problems, 1.5 s against 4.0 s in all, where the dense QP is faster still, and on ball with the exact Hessian below.)

  • Supply the exact Hessian if you can. It cut the iterations by 1.4–7×, and reached the full tolerances where L-BFGS stopped at the acceptable ones. The more degrees of freedom, the bigger the gain (next section). Its sparsity pattern has to be given too (Exact Hessian); a nonconvex Hessian is handled automatically, with a diagonal shift (found most reliably with inertia control, with MUMPS as the sparse solver if your build has it).
  • L-SR1 is competitive at this size (it took the fewest iterations of the quasi-Newton options on all three, tied on ball), unlike on the small problems. If L-BFGS converges slowly on a large problem, it is worth a try.
  • A short L-BFGS memory made each iteration cheaper without costing iterations here (5 pairs halved the time on elliptic), but cost iterations on the small problems. Try it when the QP time dominates.
  • Keep the warm start. Starting each QP from the previous one's working set cut the QP iterations 2.5–4× on elliptic and chain (and the time 2–5×).
  • Raise max_iter. Its default, 100, is enough for every problem on this page, but large problems solved with a quasi-Newton Hessian can need several hundred iterations. The tests here use 2000–3000; a solve stops as soon as it converges anyway.

Degrees of freedom

At a solution, the active constraints and bounds fix the variables in all but \( n - n_{active} \) directions: the degrees of freedom. Near a solution, SQP is a Newton-like method in those directions only. The number of degrees of freedom decides two things:

  • How much the quasi-Newton Hessian has to learn. L-BFGS learns the curvature in the free directions from one step per iteration, so many degrees of freedom with very different curvatures take many iterations. The exact Hessian has the curvature from the start.
  • How large the QP's reduced problem is. In the sparse QP, the degrees of freedom are the superbasic variables. A face with at most dense_max_ns (50) of them is solved directly, with a dense Cholesky factorization of the reduced Hessian; larger ones by conjugate gradients.

To isolate this, example/settings_study.f90 solves the same optimal-control problem (1000 time steps, 1001 equality constraints) with \( K \) piecewise-constant controls, so it has \( K \) degrees of freedom. Iterations / total time:

  • The exact Hessian's iteration count doesn't depend on the degrees of freedom (8 throughout); L-BFGS's does. On this problem it grew only from about 30 to 35, because its reduced Hessian is well conditioned. On less regular problems the gap is much larger: 47 against 7 iterations on elliptic (256 degrees of freedom), and 143 against 491 iterations between 100 and 10 pairs on TP302 (100 degrees of freedom).
  • Many degrees of freedom: use the exact Hessian if you have it. Otherwise keep the automatic L-BFGS memory, which grows with n up to 100 pairs, or raise lbfgs_memory if the solve takes more iterations than that. Try L-SR1 too.
  • Few degrees of freedom: the constraints do most of the work, and any Hessian converges quickly. A very short memory hurt here (10 pairs: 51 iterations against 29, at 5 degrees of freedom), so keep the automatic one.
  • Leave dense_max_ns alone. The direct solve costs \( O(n_s^3) \) per face. Raising the limit to 1000 made the solve up to 70× slower (at 1000 degrees of freedom) for no gain in iterations, and lowering it to 0 changed nothing. The default of 50 only takes the direct route where it is cheap.

Derivatives

If you can't supply analytic first derivatives, gjac can compute them by finite differences. On the HS problems (function evaluations of the differences are counted separately from fc; both are summed over the solved problems):

  • Central differences are nearly as good as analytic derivatives (279 problems solved against 281), at \( 2n \) extra evaluations per gradient.
  • Forward differences halve that cost, but their error (about the square root of machine precision) can keep the solver from meeting tight tolerances: two problems failed.
  • The fast/accurate switch gets most of both: it asks gjac for fast derivatives (compute forward differences when the accuracy argument is sqpopt_derivatives_fast) until it is near a solution, then for accurate ones (central differences). It needed 41% fewer evaluations than central differences throughout, and solved the same problems. See Fast derivatives.
  • Exact second derivatives pay off on large problems with many degrees of freedom (above). On small problems, L-BFGS does as well.

When the defaults fail

Set options%print_level = 1 (one line per iteration, with flags for its events) or 3 (also the QP solves, line-search trials, and restoration phases) to see what happens. Then, in rough order of how often they help:

  1. Check the scaling. Variables that differ by orders of magnitude are the most common cause of slow convergence or failure. The objective and constraints are scaled automatically, the variables are not. See Scaling.
  2. Check the derivatives, against finite differences at a few points. A wrong gradient typically shows as line-search failures (F flags) or sqpopt_line_search_failed near a solution.
  3. Stopped at max_iter, still improving: raise max_iter, and supply the exact Hessian if you can.
  4. Stopped with sqpopt_acceptable or sqpopt_stalled: the point satisfies looser tolerances, which is often all the accuracy a quasi-Newton Hessian can reach on a large problem. The exact Hessian usually reaches the full tolerances.
  5. Many restoration steps (R/P flags) or sqpopt_infeasible: the iterates have trouble getting feasible. Try a better starting point first. restoration_mode = sqpopt_restoration_gauss_newton is a cheaper alternative to the restoration phase that occasionally does better.
  6. Try another globalization. The funnel line search (linesearch_mode = sqpopt_linesearch_funnel) is as robust as the default filter on the HS set and takes a different path, so it can succeed where the filter doesn't. The trust region needs the fewest evaluations on the HS set but solves fewer problems. The Performance table compares them all.
  7. A local solution that isn't the one you want: SQP finds a local solution near the start. Different settings (the exact Hessian, another globalization) take different paths and may end at different local solutions, as do different starting points.

  • The defaults do better than every look-alike here. SQPOPT's default (the filter line search with L-BFGS) solves more of these problems, with fewer evaluations, than any of the configurations above. Use the table to reproduce a behaviour you rely on, or to compare, not as a recommendation.
  • Dense BFGS. SQPOPT has no dense BFGS matrix. Its L-BFGS memory is \( \max(10, \min(n, 100)) \) pairs by default, so on a problem with up to 100 variables it keeps every pair until the first reset, and behaves like the dense update. For a larger problem solved elsewhere with dense BFGS, raise options%lbfgs_memory.
  • SLSQP itself, run on the same problems from the same starting points, solves 262, with 18 local solutions and 25 failures (11,468 fc calls): see the results of every problem. The look-alike settings do better than that because the rest of SQPOPT is still at work: the elastic QP, the second-order correction, the restoration.
  • SNOPT. SQPOPT's sparse QP follows the design of SNOPT's QP solver, SQOPT (a basis factorization and a reduced Hessian), and its elastic mode is the same idea. Its differences, from the SNOPT user's guide: it keeps the linear constraints satisfied at every iterate, so the functions are only evaluated where those hold (SQPOPT guarantees that only for the bounds on the variables); its limited-memory matrix is a diagonal with at most 10 updates, which are discarded back to the diagonal when the limit is reached (SQPOPT keeps the newest pairs, up to 100 by default: set options%lbfgs_memory = 10 to come closer); once it enters elastic mode it stays in it for the rest of the solve; and its QP can keep a Cholesky or a quasi-Newton factor of the reduced Hessian, where SQPOPT's uses conjugate gradients or a dense factor rebuilt for each face. The sparse QP is SQPOPT's default for more than 200 variables; the setting in the table forces it for small problems too.
  • filterSQP. It solves nonconvex QPs with the exact Hessian; SQPOPT shifts an indefinite Hessian instead (see Inertia control). The trust region is SQPOPT's weakest globalization on this problem set: prefer the default line search unless you need a trust region.
  • IPOPT. There is no interior-point method in SQPOPT. The settings in the table share IPOPT's ingredients (they are the fastest configuration on large problems), but the methods scale differently. An interior-point iteration costs one factorization however many inequality constraints change their activity; SQPOPT's direct QP is at its best when the active set changes little from one iteration to the next. An active-set method, on the other hand, restarts well from a nearby solution (lambda0, and the QP's working set), which an interior-point method does not. IPOPT with hessian_approximation = limited-memory corresponds to SQPOPT's default L-BFGS, with direct_qp on large problems.
  • Uno builds its methods from parts, as SQPOPT's options do. Its trust-region funnel method corresponds to linesearch_mode = sqpopt_linesearch_funnel with trust_region%enabled = .true. (263 / 40 / 2 with 10,676 here, with L-BFGS).
  • No counterpart. filterSD (Fletcher) uses no Hessian approximation at all, and OPTGRA (ESA) is a gradient-projection method: SQPOPT has no settings like them. The nearest to filterSD in structure is the trust region with the filter and L-BFGS (263 / 39 / 3 with 9,251).

How these were measured

Every number on this page can be reproduced with a release build:

# small problems: the HS suite with a configuration option
# (e.g. --qp=sparse, --qp=sparse-lsqr, --hessian=sr1, --hessian=exact,
#  --lbfgs-memory=10, --derivatives=central, --derivatives=forward, --derivatives=fast)
fpm test test_hs_suite --profile release -- /dev/null --qp=sparse

# large sparse problems: every sparse-QP and Hessian setting
fpm test test_sparse_options --profile release

# the QP solver against size, and the degrees-of-freedom study
fpm run --example settings_study --profile release

The test problems are deliberately regular (smooth, well scaled, with unique solutions), so that each setting's effect is measurable. Your problem may behave differently. When it matters, try the two or three most promising settings from this page on your own problem, and compare the iterations, results%n_eval_fc, and results%time_qp.
